# B. Sculpture and generative art: few intentional lines

Research for the NESS barbed-wire wave (Three.js, ink #17150f, cream #e9e1ce, red #c0230b).
Problem: about 46 cross-section loops read as concertina coil, wire mesh or a wireframe.

**Access note.** "Fetched" means I loaded the page text with WebFetch. "Search-result only" means the facts come from search snippets and I did not open the page. I saw no images at all, so the visual descriptions below come from the text sources plus well-known facts about each work. Check them against photographs before relying on them.

## 1. Curated references

### 1. Naum Gabo, *Linear Construction in Space No. 1 / No. 2* (1942–1950s)
- URLs: https://www.nga.gov/artworks/73447-linear-construction-space-no-2 (fetch got a 403; search-result only). https://www.math.brown.edu/tbanchof/Beyond3D.new/chapter7/s7_6.html (fetched). https://www.stedelijk.nl/en/collection/2157-naum-gabo-linear-construction-in-space-no.-2 (search-result only).
- Visual qualities: nylon monofilament stretched over two intersecting Perspex lobes. The threads are straight and tight, yet together they make a curved, glowing surface that twists into a saddle-like void. The Brown page describes it as segments in the "configuration space of segments" whose sequence lies on a hyperbolic paraboloid.
- Relevance: this is the clearest case of a curved surface built entirely from straight tensioned lines. A wave face is a twisting saddle, rising and pitching forward.
- Principle: **ruled surface.** Take two rails (base and lip). A point moves along each rail at its own speed, and a straight chord joins the pair. The twist comes from the phase offset between the two rails. Around 20–40 chords look like a surface because the eye fills in the gaps. The chords converge where the rails come close, so density gathers on its own at the curl.
- Don't copy: the evenly spaced, harp-like density. It looks like a lab model. Also avoid enclosing the form in a frame.

### 2. Barbara Hepworth, string sculptures and *Winged Figure* (1957–63)
- URLs: https://artmuselondon.com/2025/04/27/self-taut-barbara-hepworth-strings-piano-nobile/ (fetched). https://piano-nobile.com/exhibitions/119-barbara-hepworth-strings (search-result only).
- Visual qualities: strings cross a hollow or join two bladed wings. Sources describe them as "bursting out from the centre" and guiding the eye around the piece. A solid mass is paired with taut line.
- Principle: **strings belong where there is tension between two solid edges.** They mark a void; they do not fill it. On the wave, one heavy stroke (the lip) plus a few strings to the base reads better than an even weave.
- Don't copy: the modernist stone and bronze mass. We only have wire.

### 3. Alexander Calder, wire portraits and *Josephine Baker* figures (c. 1926–31)
- URLs: https://calder.org/works/wire-sculpture/josephine-baker-ii-c-1927 (fetched, text only). https://whitney.org/collection/works/2784 (search-result only; the Whitney calls them "continuous line drawings" in 3D).
- Visual qualities: one line that switches roles between contour, interior feature and spring-like volume. Long empty spans sit between small, dense passages. The shadow is part of the drawing.
- Principle: **continuous gesture with role changes.** A single path can trace the silhouette, then turn inward to give one interior cue (the eye of the barrel), then leave. Calder's figures read because he drew only the contours that carry information.
- Don't copy: the cartoonish caricature. And watch for this: the Baker figures use tight coils for the torso, and that coil is exactly the look we're trying to escape.

### 4. Ruth Asawa, looped-wire hanging forms (1950s on)
- URLs: https://50x50.sjmusart.org/asawa/asawa1/ (fetch got a 403; search-result only). https://www.guggenheim-bilbao.eus/en/exhibition/vocabulary-of-my-sculpture (search-result only).
- Visual qualities: a crocheted wire skin making lobed hourglass shapes. Forms nest inside forms, "orbs that seem to float within larger spheres". The skins are transparent enough that the inner shape shows through. In her words, she took a line and moved it into space.
- Principle: **nested transparent volumes.** Put a smaller inner form (the tube or barrel) inside the outer silhouette. Depth comes from two outlines overlapping, and you don't need a lattice for it.
- Don't copy: the looped mesh skin. On screen it becomes the generic mesh texture we're trying to escape.

### 5. Reuben Margolin, kinetic wave sculptures (*Magic Wave*, *Confluence*, *Filament*)
- URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuben_Heyday_Margolin (fetched). https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/monstershop2015/2015/09/10/machine-research-project-kinetic-wave-sculptures/ (fetched). https://www.reubenmargolin.com/ (fetched, but no construction text). Search snippets put *Magic Wave* at 256 cables and a combination of four variable-amplitude waves.
- Visual qualities: hundreds of hanging points whose heights are the sum of a few sinusoids. You see the wave as the envelope of the points; no single element is a wave.
- Principle: **the wave is a field of phase, not a shape.** If each station along a wire gets a height from sin(kx − ωt + φ), the form moves like water. This is the strongest model for scroll motion: scroll drives ωt.
- Don't copy: the grid of many hanging elements. With barbs, it becomes a fence or curtain.

### 6. Richard Serra, *Torqued Ellipses* (1996 on)
- URLs: https://www.moma.org/audio/playlist/236/3051 (search-result only). https://thelonelypalette.com/episode-8-serra (search-result only).
- Visual qualities: the top ellipse is the same as the bottom one, only rotated, so the wall leans over you on one side and away from you on the other. One continuous surface, with no ribs.
- Principle: **same curve top and bottom, rotated: the lean is the drama.** Build the wave face as one profile curve swept and rotated about 30–60° from base to crest. That is also a ruled surface, and the overhang of the lip comes for free.
- Don't copy: the solid plate. Our surface has to stay implied by lines.

### 7. Gabriel Dawe, *Plexus* series
- URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plexus_A1 (search-result only). https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/artist-gabriel-dawe-used-60-miles-embroider-thread-make-rainbow-180957243/ (search-result only).
- Visual qualities: thread hooked between ceiling and floor rails, laid in layers, often crossing as twisted sheets. Up close it dissolves; from the right angle it becomes a solid prism of light.
- Principle: **viewpoint-dependent solidity.** A ruled sheet looks solid when you see it edge-on and almost vanishes face-on. Pick the hero camera so the sheet sits near edge-on at the lip, where it reads as a bright crest line.
- Don't copy: the miles of thread. Dawe gets density from thousands of strands, and we can't.

### 8. Janet Echelman, aerial net sculptures
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Echelman (search-result only).
- Visual qualities: huge nets whose surfaces "reveal every ripple of wind" and keep changing shape.
- Relevance: a mostly negative lesson. Her forms read because they are large, coloured and lit. A net in a dark Three.js scene is just mesh. The useful part is that motion reveals form.
- Don't copy: the net topology.

### 9. Ned Kahn, *Wind Veil* / *Turbulent Line* facades
- URLs: https://www.uapcompany.com/projects/turbulent-line (search-result only). https://claycorp.com/project/ned-kahn-wind-wall-at-centene-plaza (search-result only).
- Visual qualities: tens of thousands of hinged aluminium flaps. Gusts show up as travelling waves of glitter.
- Principle: **the barbs as the "flaps".** The barbs could catch the light (cream specular glints) in a moving band that runs along a still wire, so the wave's travel shows in the highlights without the geometry moving. That is cheap and elegant motion.
- Don't copy: the full-surface pixel grid.

### 10. Lungiswa Gqunta, *Ntabamanzi* (Henry Moore Institute)
- URL: https://henry-moore.org/seasons/lungiswa-gqunta-sleep-in-witness (fetched; caption only).
- Facts: barbed wire with blue wool, installed against a gallery wall to read as a wave breaking against it. It is the only barbed-wire wave I found.
- Relevance: a direct precedent and conceptual warning. Barbed wire brings meanings of border, violence and dispossession. Gqunta uses that charge on purpose; NESS should know the precedent exists. I couldn't get its construction details.
- Don't copy: the wall-crash composition or the wool pairing.

### 11. Melvin Edwards, barbed-wire installations (1969–70, re-shown at Dia Beacon 2022)
- URL: https://www.stephenfriedman.com/news/684-melvin-edwards/ (search-result only). The Alexander Gray page returned a 404.
- Visual qualities: barbed wire strung across rooms in sparse lines and drapes. The search sources describe "punctuated lines" that make depth through linearity.
- Principle: **the barb is punctuation.** Barbs make a dotted rhythm along a line. In a few long lines that rhythm reads as a measured, intentional mark. Packed into many short loops, it reads as concertina.
- Don't copy: the room-spanning installation logic.

### 12. Hokusai, *Under the Wave off Kanagawa* (c. 1831)
- URLs: https://www.christies.com.cn/en/lot/lot-6137379 (search-result only: the claw-like crests are "cresting waves that end in hooks", called fractal waves). https://gauss.math.yale.edu/public_html/People/frame/Fractals/Panorama/Art/Hokusai/Hokusai.html (search-result only).
- Visual qualities: one dominant spiral (the barrel), a thick outer contour, and inner parallel contour lines following the face. Detail is concentrated only at the lip, as claws that repeat at smaller scales.
- Principle: **hierarchy: one spine curve, a few contour lines, detail only at the crest.** The barbs belong where Hokusai puts the claws: dense at the lip, sparse or absent on the face and trough.
- Don't copy: the literal claws or the boats. That's pastiche.

### 13. Ensō (Zen single-stroke circle)
- URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ens%C5%8D (search-result only). https://collections.artsmia.org/art/60220 (search-result only; it notes the stroke becomes scratchier as the brush runs out of ink).
- Principle: **open, imperfect, one or two strokes, with a visible start and end.** The stroke thins and breaks as it dries. Translation: vary the wire's apparent weight along its length, thickest at the gesture's start and frayed or thinning at the lip, and leave the curl open.
- Don't copy: the closed circle or the calligraphy texture as a shader trick.

### 14. Tyler Hobbs, flow fields (essay) and Anders Hoff / inconvergent, *Differential Line*
- URLs: https://www.tylerxhobbs.com/words/flow-fields (fetched). https://inconvergent.net (search-result only, via secondary summaries).
- Hobbs's method (fetched): step curves through an angle field, keep spacing with collision checks, and use long curves for fluid "leading lines". Objects placed in the field deflect it, the way a fluid wraps around an obstacle.
- Principle: **streamlines with a minimum separation.** Seed 3–7 lines and integrate them through a 3D wave velocity field (orbital motion that rises up the face, pitches over and curls). The collision rule stops them crowding. Hoff's growth adds nodes where curvature is highest: put geometric detail (barbs, twist) where the curvature is.
- Don't copy: Perlin noise fields (Hobbs himself calls them overused) or the full-page hatching density.

### 15. Anti-references: Edoardo Tresoldi (wire-mesh "ghost" architecture), David Moreno (cross-hatched wire drawings), Calatrava's ribs
- URLs: https://domusweb.it/en/architecture/gallery/2018/11/06/interview-edoardo-tresoldi.html (search-result only). https://mymodernmet.com/david-moreno-drawing-sculptures (search-result only). https://calatrava.com/projects/turning-torso-malmoe.html (search-result only).
- Tresoldi's mesh "holds space without occupying it". It works only at architectural scale with daylight behind it. On a dark screen, mesh just reads as a wireframe render.
- Moreno uses cross-hatching for shading, and sources note that the form "disappears into the mass" as you walk around it. That is exactly our failure mode.
- Calatrava (Oculus ribs, the Turning Torso's 1.6°-per-floor twist with an external spine) is the useful exception. Ribs work when they are **few, hung off a dominant spine, and change progressively** (rotation, length) so they read as one gesture. Ours fail because the 46 loops are many, closed and have no spine.

Also checked: Anthony Howe (search-result only, https://www.thisiscolossal.com/2013/06/hypnotic-wind-powered-kinetic-sculptures-by-anthony-howe/). His welded stainless armatures of curved repeated blades show that **controlled repetition rotating about an axis** reads as motion, not mesh, but only because each element is a curved blade with a clear shared axis. I didn't verify Zimoun, Shlian, Saraceno, Park, Mitsuya, Yamashita, Anadol, Hadid, Frei Otto, Bufalino or Lieberman's single-line work in this pass.

## 2. Construction strategies for a wire wave

**A. One gesture stroke (Calder / ensō).**
- One continuous wire, up to about 2 strands twisted. It starts low in the trough, rises up the face, pitches over as the lip, curls into an open barrel, and exits as a spent tail.
- Silhouette: a cresting hook, like the letter "e" or a 6.
- Reads best in side profile. At three-quarter view it needs a slight z-offset in the curl so it doesn't flatten.
- Mesh risk: very low. Coil risk: low, provided the curl makes at most 1.25 turns.
- Motion: scroll draws the stroke (drawRange or a progress uniform), or the curl closes and opens.

**B. Ruled surface between two rails (Gabo / Serra / Dawe).**
- A base rail along the trough and a lip rail that leans forward and rotates about 40° (Serra's torque). Join them with 14–28 straight chords, plus both rails drawn heavy.
- Silhouette: a twisting saddle face with an overhanging lip.
- Reads best at three-quarter view. Edge-on it collapses into a bright crest line, which is useful.
- Mesh risk: medium if the chords are evenly spaced. Space them by arc length on the *lip* so they fan and converge.
- Motion: slide the chord phase offset with scroll, so the surface twists and rolls like the wave peeling.

**C. Nested contour lines (Hokusai / topographic).**
- 3–5 open, roughly parallel curves follow the face, each offset inward, shorter and lower than the last. The outermost is the heaviest and the only one with barbs at the lip.
- Silhouette: a layered crest that echoes itself.
- Reads best side-on. In 3D, give each line its own z so they splay apart in perspective.
- Mesh risk: low. Never connect the contours to each other.
- Motion: stagger them, outer first, as a travelling phase.

**D. Variable-pitch helix (coil done right).**
- One helix along the wave axis, with pitch and radius as functions of position. In the trough the pitch is so long it is nearly a straight line; at the barrel it tightens to 2–3 turns; it opens out again after.
- Silhouette: a tube that only exists at the barrel.
- Reads best end-on, looking down the barrel. Side-on it is risky.
- Coil risk: high. Use only if the pitch spans at least 10:1 and there are no more than 3 tight turns.
- Motion: scroll pushes the tight zone along the axis, so the barrel travels.

**E. Streamlines over the form (Hobbs / Hoff).**
- Integrate 4–7 lines through an analytic wave velocity field, seeded at different heights in the trough. A minimum-separation rule terminates any line that gets too close to another. The lines fan up the face, converge at the lip and spill.
- Silhouette: wind-swept hair forming a crest.
- Reads well from any camera, the most robust of the seven.
- Mesh risk: low, as long as nothing crosses.
- Motion: advect a dash or barb-glint pattern along the lines (Ned Kahn), or re-integrate with time-varying phase (Margolin).

**F. Sparse ribs on a spine (Calatrava).**
- One heavy spine curve along the crest, plus 5–9 *open* ribs (C-shapes, not loops) hanging off it at key stations only: trough, face, lip, barrel and spill. Each rib rotates progressively relative to the last.
- Silhouette: a vertebral wave.
- Reads best at three-quarter view.
- Mesh risk: medium, rising sharply above 9 ribs or if the ribs close.
- Motion: the ribs fan open in sequence, or rotate (the 1.6°-per-floor logic, scaled up).

**G. Silhouette plus one depth line.**
- A drawn profile in the x–y plane plus one line running into z along the lip, so 2 wires. Literally a drawing with one 3D cue.
- Silhouette: the most graphic and logo-like.
- Reads best from the front and side, then falls apart past about 45°. Lock the camera orbit.
- Mesh risk: none.
- Motion: the depth line travels along z (peel), or camera parallax shows the second line.

**Recommended combination: G or A as the hero line, plus B with no more than 20 chords or E with no more than 6 lines as the supporting surface. Barbs go only on the hero line, concentrated at the lip.**

## 3. Rules for avoiding the mesh / coil / wireframe look

1. **Line budget: 1 hero wire, plus no more than 7 supporting lines or 20 chords.** If you can't count the lines at a glance, there are too many.
2. **No closed loops.** Closed cross-sections are the signature of both wireframe and concertina. Every curve has a start and an end, and the ends are visible.
3. **Hierarchy of weight.** The hero line is 2–3 times thicker or brighter than the support lines, and support lines are dimmer still (cream at 40–60% against the ink). Equal weight means mesh.
4. **Barbs are punctuation, not texture.** Concentrate them where curvature is high (the lip and the claw). Use no more than about 25 barbs in total, and leave long bare spans. A barb at a constant interval everywhere looks like fence.
5. **Uneven spacing, on purpose.** Space the chords and ribs by a curvature or arc-length rule so they converge at the curl and open on the face. Uniform spacing reads as a CAD wireframe.
6. **No crossings in the hero view.** Lines may converge and nearly touch, but crossings make a lattice.
7. **Keep the curl open and the turns at no more than 1.25.** More turns make a spring. If you do use a helix, the pitch varies by at least 10:1.
8. **Silhouette first.** Test the form as a flat black shape. If the outline doesn't say "breaking wave" without the inner lines, add nothing inside; fix the outline.
9. **Negative space is the volume.** Leave the barrel empty, with no lines inside the tube. The void is what Hepworth and Gabo use to make the form.
10. **Choose the hero camera and design for it.** Ruled sheets go edge-on at the lip (Dawe), and contours splay in perspective. Limit the orbit to about ±35° so the drawing never degenerates.
11. **One red accent with meaning.** Red (#c0230b) marks a single event: the lip, the point where the wave breaks, or the single barb at the claw. Red spread across many elements turns into decoration.
12. **Motion is a phase field, not a deformation of everything.** Scroll drives one travelling phase (Margolin): the curl peels, the glints run, or the stroke draws on. Avoid uniform jiggle or noise on every vertex.
13. **Taper and fray.** Vary the apparent thickness or opacity along the hero wire (ensō dry-brush): strong at the root, thinning toward the spilling tail.
14. **No lighting that outlines every segment.** Fresnel rim on every tube equals a wireframe render. Use one key light with glints on the barbs only, and let most of the wire fall into the ink.
15. **Know the barbed-wire charge** (Gqunta, Edwards). With sparse, gestural lines the material reads as a drawing that has been made deliberate. Dense coils bring back its connotation of borders and razor wire.
